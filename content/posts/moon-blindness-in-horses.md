## Moon blindness, also known as Recurrent Uveitis, is one of the most common causes of blindness in horses. 


⁠⁠It is a painful eye condition that causes repeated episodes of inflammation, which can eventually lead to vision loss. While it has been known for centuries, the exact causes are still not completely understood. However, research suggests that infections, an overactive immune system, and genetics all play a role.

### **Why Is It Called Moon Blindness?**

The name "moon blindness" comes from an old belief that the condition was linked to the phases of the moon. People thought that flare-ups occurred more often when the moon was full. Today, we know that this isn’t true, but the name has remained. Another old name for it was "periodic ophthalmia," which refers to the way the condition comes and goes in episodes.

### **Understanding the Horse’s Eye**

To understand moon blindness, it's helpful to know a little about the structure of the eye. The part of the eye that becomes inflamed is called the uveal tract. This includes the iris (the colored part of the eye), the ciliary body (which helps focus the lens), and the choroid (which supplies blood to the retina). When this area becomes inflamed, it can cause pain, swelling, and other problems that interfere with a horse's vision.

### **What Causes Moon Blindness?**

Scientists don’t yet know exactly why some horses develop moon blindness, but several factors seem to contribute:

- **Bacterial Infections:** One of the most common infections linked to this condition comes from bacteria called *Leptospira*. This bacteria can enter the horse's body through contaminated water, food, or soil. If it gets into the eye, it can trigger an immune response that leads to inflammation.
- **The Immune System:** Many researchers believe that moon blindness is an autoimmune disease. This means that, for some reason, the horse's immune system starts attacking its own eye tissues, causing repeated inflammation.
- **Genetics:** Some horse breeds, especially Appaloosas, are much more likely to develop moon blindness. Studies show that Appaloosas have up to eight times the risk of developing the condition compared to other breeds. This suggests that there is a genetic factor involved.

### **Symptoms of Moon Blindness**

Moon blindness can affect one or both eyes and may come and go. Some of the most common signs include:

- Squinting or keeping the eye closed
- Excessive tearing
- Redness in the eye
- Cloudiness or a bluish-white haze over the eye
- Sensitivity to light
- A shrunken or irregularly shaped pupil

In the early stages, these symptoms may seem to improve on their own, but the condition usually returns. Over time, repeated inflammation can cause permanent damage, leading to cataracts, glaucoma, or complete blindness.

### **How Is It Diagnosed?**

A veterinarian will usually diagnose moon blindness by examining the horse’s eye and looking for signs of past inflammation. They may use tools like an ophthalmoscope to check for damage inside the eye or a tonometer to measure eye pressure. If *Leptospira* bacteria are suspected, the vet may also take a blood or eye fluid sample for testing.

### **Treatment Options**

While there is no cure for moon blindness, early treatment can help manage symptoms and slow down the progression of the disease. The main goals of treatment are to reduce inflammation, control pain, and protect the eye from further damage.

- **Medications:** Anti-inflammatory drugs like corticosteroids or NSAIDs (such as Banamine) are often used to reduce swelling and pain. If bacteria are involved, antibiotics may also be prescribed. Atropine is sometimes used to dilate the pupil and relieve discomfort.
- **Surgery:** In severe cases, surgery may be an option. A procedure called vitrectomy, where the fluid inside the eye is removed and replaced, has shown success in reducing flare-ups. Another option is an implant that slowly releases medication to control inflammation.
- **Protective Gear:** Horses with moon blindness are often very sensitive to light, so using fly masks or other protective eyewear can help keep them comfortable.

### **What Is the Long-Term Outlook?**

The long-term outcome for a horse with moon blindness depends on how early the condition is caught and how well it is managed. Some horses only experience mild flare-ups and can live relatively normal lives with proper treatment. However, in more severe cases, repeated inflammation can cause permanent blindness.

If a horse does lose its vision, it can still adapt and lead a good life with extra care. Blind horses can often rely on their other senses, and they usually do best when kept in a familiar environment with a consistent routine.

### **Can Moon Blindness Be Prevented?**

There is no guaranteed way to prevent moon blindness, but there are some steps owners can take to reduce the risk:

- **Limit Exposure to Infections:** Since *Leptospira* bacteria are linked to the condition, keeping water sources clean and reducing exposure to stagnant or contaminated water can help.
- **Regular Eye Checks:** Catching the disease early gives the best chance of slowing its progression. If a horse shows any signs of eye discomfort, a vet should check it as soon as possible.
- **Protecting Sensitive Eyes:** Using a UV-blocking fly mask can help protect the eyes from bright sunlight and irritants, which might help reduce flare-ups.

### **Final Thoughts**

Moon blindness is a challenging condition for both horses and their owners. While there is no cure, early detection and proper management can help slow down the disease and keep affected horses comfortable. Understanding the causes, symptoms, and treatment options can make a big difference in caring for a horse with this condition. Research continues to improve treatments, and with the right care, many horses with moon blindness can still live happy and fulfilling lives.
