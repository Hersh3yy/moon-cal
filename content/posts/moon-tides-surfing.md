Surfing is as much about reading the ocean as it is about skill. A great surfer knows that waves aren't just random—they are shaped by powerful natural forces, including the gravitational pull of the moon. Understanding how tides work can help surfers, from beginners to pros, catch the best waves and enhance their experience in the water.

## Does the moon affects tides for surfing?

The moon plays a crucial role in generating tides, which directly influence the size and shape of the waves surfers ride. The gravitational pull of the moon (and to a lesser extent, the sun) creates a rhythmic rise and fall in sea levels. These changes in water levels are known as tides.

### The Science of Tides

There are two primary forces at play:

1. **Gravitational Pull of the Moon** – The moon’s gravity pulls on Earth's water, causing a bulge or high tide on the side of the Earth facing the moon.
2. **Centrifugal Force** – As the Earth and moon rotate around a common center of mass, an opposing bulge forms on the side opposite the moon, creating another high tide.

Because of these forces, most coastal areas experience two high tides and two low tides each day, known as **semi-diurnal tides**. Some places, however, experience only one high and one low tide daily (**diurnal tides**).

### Spring Tides vs. Neap Tides

- **Spring Tides** occur when the sun, moon, and Earth are aligned during full and new moons. These tides produce the highest highs and lowest lows, creating more powerful waves.
- **Neap Tides** happen when the sun and moon form a right angle with the Earth, during the first and third quarter moons. These tides result in smaller tidal variations and generally weaker waves.

### How Tides Affect Surfing Conditions

Tides influence the depth of water over the ocean floor, affecting wave shape and surf conditions. Some breaks work best at high tide, while others come alive at low tide. Understanding these variations can make the difference between an epic surf session and a frustrating one.

#### High Tide Surfing

Pros:

- Often smoother, slower waves, making it great for longboarders.
- Works well for deep-water reef breaks.
- Reduced risk of hitting rocks or reefs in shallow areas.

Cons:

- Waves may become "fat" or slow down.
- Some beach breaks may close out (waves break all at once, making them unsurfable).

#### Low Tide Surfing

Pros:

- Steeper, more hollow waves, perfect for barrels.
- ⁠Works well for sandbar and reef breaks.
- Offers faster, more powerful waves for experienced surfers.

Cons:

- Shallow waters can be hazardous, especially near reefs.
- Stronger currents can make paddling more difficult.

#### Mid-Tide Surfing

Pros:

- Often provides the best compromise between wave shape and power.
- Many surf spots perform best during the rising or falling tide.
- Suitable for a wide range of skill levels.

Cons:

- Conditions can change rapidly, requiring adaptability.

### Best Tide Conditions for Different Skill Levels

#### Beginner Surfers: Mid to High Tide

If you're just starting, look for mellow waves with a slow, rolling break. High tide is often the safest option as it provides softer waves, reducing the risk of hitting the ocean floor. Beach breaks are a great choice, especially on days with smaller swells.

Ideal conditions:

- Small, slow waves (1-3 feet)
- Soft beach breaks
- Mid to high tide to avoid shallow hazards

#### **Intermediate Surfers: Rising Mid-Tide**

Intermediate surfers can handle slightly larger and steeper waves but still benefit from forgiving conditions. A rising mid-tide often produces clean, well-formed waves without the intensity of low tide barrels. This is a great time to work on turns, trimming, and speed control.

Ideal conditions:

- Moderate waves (3-6 feet)
- Point or reef breaks with a consistent shape
- Rising mid-tide for balanced power and control

#### **Pro Surfers: Low to Mid-Tide on Larger Swells**

Advanced surfers thrive in challenging conditions, including steep, barreling waves and fast breaks. Low tide at reef and point breaks provides the perfect setup for deep tubes and critical maneuvers.

Ideal conditions:

- Powerful, fast waves (6+ feet)
- Hollow barrels and steep take-offs
- Low tide at reef or point breaks for maximum challenge

### **Other Factors Influencing Surf Conditions**

While tides are crucial, other factors also shape the surf experience:

#### **Wind**

- **Offshore wind (blowing from land to sea)** creates clean, well-formed waves.
- **Onshore wind (blowing from sea to land)** makes waves choppy and messy.
- **No wind** provides glassy, smooth conditions.

#### **Swell Direction and Size**

- Different breaks respond best to specific swell directions.
- A larger swell generally means bigger, more powerful waves.

#### **Local Bathymetry (Ocean Floor Shape)**

- Sandbars, reefs, and underwater structures influence how waves break.
- Some breaks work best at a particular tide due to their underwater topography.

### **Best part about Surfing? It is scientifically proven that it makes you feel better**

In his book, [High Surf, author Tim Baker](https://amzn.to/3DAITla), a good friend of the GSI family, interviewed scientist Vezen Wu who was at Columbia University at the time. Vezen was convinced that the scientific explanation for the feelings of elation he experienced while surfing were due to the existence of negative ions from breaking waves, which had been proven in clinical experiments to elevate a person's mood.

Positive ions attract atmospheric pollutants, dust particles and harmful airborne matter. They are high in indoor work environments where computer screens and TVs are rampant. Negative ions, on the other hand, cancel out the effect of positive ions and clean the air, increasing the sense of wellbeing.

Since the ocean surf is one of the largest natural sources of negative ions, created from water molecules rubbing over each other when waves break, Vezen hypothesized that a surfer's stoke could be explained by being immersed the ocean, surrounded by breaking waves which would release negative ions and could elevate mood by affecting brain chemistry.
